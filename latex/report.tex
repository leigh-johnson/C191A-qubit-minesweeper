\subsection{Noise Models}
\paragraphsc{Pauli Noise.}
The simplest model used to evaluate Quantum Error Correction (QEC) assumes all physical qubit errors can be represented by uniformly random Pauli operators applied during circuit simulation. In our simulations we implement a \gls{depolarizing error channel} to represent \gls{Pauli noise}, which has the following effect on some quantum state $\rho$ with $p$ probability of becoming a maximally-mixed state and $(1-p)$ probability of remaining unechanged:
\begin{align}E_{\text{pauli}}(\rho) & = (1-p)\rho + \dfrac{p}{3}(X\rho X + Y\rho Y + Z\rho Z) & \quad \text{[Depolarizing error channel]}
\end{align}

Unfortunately, error models based only on \gls{Pauli noise} are not robust enough to describe the real-world noise that must be corrected to realize fault-tolerant quantum computation. [CITATION NEEDED]

\paragraphsc{Leakage.}
A \gls{leakage} occurs when a qubit ``leaks'' out of the computational subspace (described by a two-dimensional Hilbert space) into a state that can only be described with a higher-order Hilbert space. If leakage goes undetected, the error compounds over time as it spreads through a multi-qubit interactions. Leakage can be stochastically modeled with a 3-level system (qutrit) with orthonormal basis: $\set{\ket{0}, \ket{1}, \ket{2}}$. Leakage events are assumed to be discrete and occur independently with probability $p_{\text{leak}}$ at each gate operation acting on the qutrit \cite{suchara_leakage_2014}. The leakage error $E_{E_{\uparrow}}$ on the $j$th qutrit in the system can then be understood as:
\begin{align}E_{\text{leakage}}(\rho_j) & = (1-p_{\text{leak}})\rho_j + p_{\text{leak}}\ket{2}\bra{2} & \quad \text{[Leakage error channel \cite{suchara_leakage_2014}]}
\end{align}

Leakage-reduction units (LRUs) were proposed as a way to convert leakage errors into Pauli-type errors, based on a one-bit teleportation protocol that effectively moves the logical state from the physical qubit in the leaked state to a freshly-prepared physical qubit \cite{suchara_leakage_2014}.

All species of qubit experience leakage errors, but it's important to note the underlying \emph{physical} processes look and behave very differently despite sharing a common mathematical description.


Transmon leakage. Transmon single and two qubit gates rely on coherent microwave driving to transition between computational basis states. Two qubit gates such as the controll Z and controll X gates are the primary source of leakage in transmon qubits because of their temporary usage of states outside of the computational basis to induce phase and bit flips in the target qubit. For example, when applying a control Z gate on transmon hardware to the state {$\ket{11}$}, temporarily force both excitons onto one qubit, pushing it into the second excited state, {$\ket{2}$}, and letting the other qubit fall into the ground state, {$\ket{0}$}, before allowing the system to relax back into {$\ket{11}$} with the intended phase shift applied. However, there is a nonzero chance that the system does not relax properly, and leaves one of our qubits in the {$\ket{2}$} state, causing a leakage error in our system.
\cite{Yang_2024} \cite{Miao_2023}

Neutral atom / Rydberg leakage. Neutral atom quantum computers exploit the large effective atomic radius of Rydberg states to perform two qubit gates. When an electron is excited to a Rydberg state, the separation between the electron and the now ionized nucleus creates an electric dipole field, which shifts excitation energy of the Rydberg state, an effect called Rydberg blockade. Following the protocol from Jaksch et. al, to perform a control Z gate, apply a {$\pi$} pulse to excite the control qubit into a Rydberg state, apply a 2{$\pi$} pulse to the target qubit, and then apply a {$\pi$} pulse to deexcite the control qubit \cite{Jaksch_Fast_Quantum_gates_2000}. If both qubits are in the {$\ket{0}$} then the state will remain unchanged, but if exactly one qubit is in the {$\ket{1}$} state then a rotation by 2{$\pi$} and thus a phase shift of -1, will be applied. If both qubits are in the {$\ket{11}$} state then after the first pulse, the Rydberg blockade will prevent the second excitation and a phase of -1 will be applied. Finally apply a Pauli Z gate to both qubits to shift the states -{$\ket{01}$ and -{$\ket{10}$ to {$\ket{01}$ and {$\ket{10}$. Occasionally when applying this protocol, the Rydberg state will not decay back to the {$\ket{1}$} state, and will instead decay to an orbital state outside of our computational basis, causing a leakage error. \cite{morgado_quantum_2021}

\paragraphsc{Erasure.} An \gls{erasure} occurs when a qubit is longer detectable by the measurement device, so the quantum state information encoded by the qubit is effectively ``lost'' or ``erased''. Mathematically, we extend the qubit Hilbert space with some $\ket{e}$ that is orthogonal to the qubit state $\{\ket{0}, \ket{1}\}$. The erasure channel can be defined as:

\begin{align}E_{\text{erase}}(\rho_j) & = (1-p_{\text{erase}})\rho_j + p_{\text{erase}}\ket{e}\bra{e} & \quad \text{[Erasure error channel \cite{suchara_leakage_2014}]}
\end{align}

Photon loss is the dominant form of error in photonic qubits [CITATION NEEDED].

Leakage-detection units (LDUs) were developed to address leakage errors in superconducting qubits, by periodically coupling a data qubit to an acilla that flags when the data qubit leaves the computational subspace. The flagged data qubit can be reset/replaced during subsequent error-correction cycles, effectively treating the flagged qubit as an erasure error.

Neutral atoms / Rydberg arrays, each atom is arranged into a known position using optical tweezers. Although the lattice/trap is situated inside a vacuum chamber, some nonzero amount of background gas collisions or thermal energy transfer from the environment can eject an atom from its lattice site.

\bigskip

It is common and convenient to assume for general quantum error correcting codes that Pauli errors occur independently from each other. This assumption simplifies the decoding process, but does not accurately reflect the behavior of the real world. Experiments show that noise is often correlated through space or time in every platform. Thus, the correction of correlated errors is necessary to achieve a fault-tolerant quantum computer. \cite{wilen_correlated_2021}

\bigskip

In neutral atoms, there exists the chance of cross-talk between atoms when performing two qubit gates. Rydberg blockade is a necessary step to entangle two atoms, but applying the Rydberg excitation laser can unintentionally excite neighboring atoms. \textbf{CITATION NEEDED}

\bigskip

For superconducting transmon qubits, experiments have shown that the logical error rate can be reduced by increasing the code size of the surface code. This was done through the use of turnable couplers, which increase the fidelity of two qubit gates and prevent unwanted interaction. However, adding couplers introduces leakage errors. This is because during two qubit gates, couplers may gain extra population that stays there for a period of time. The extra population introduces time-corrolated errors, because it will affect the next gate operation. Furthermore, the extra population within the couplers may spread to other couplers, leading to space-corrolated errors. \cite{yang_coupler-assisted_2024}


\subsection{Erasure Qubits}

A qubit engineered so the dominant source of errors is from erasures.

A bias towards erasure-type errors occurs natively in some types of qubits (e.g. photons) or can be engineered by converting leakage errors to erasures (e.g. with LDUs in superconducting qubits).

\bigskip

Erasure qubits are qubits that are engineered so that the dominant error channel is erased. This means that the system can detect when and where the error happened. Some platforms exhibit a natural bias towards erasure, such as photons, which makes it easier to create erasure qubits. Other platforms however do not have a native erasure bias, but can be engineered to create one. \subsubsection{Advantages, Challenges of Erasure Errors}

\paragraphsc{Advantages.}
From: "The benefits and costs of quantum error correction with erasure qubits" (QIP 2025)
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can theoretically correct more erasure errors than unknown Pauli errors.
          We can correct $d-1$ erasure errors compared to $\dfrac{d-1}{2}$ Pauli errors. As a result, we expect logical error rate $P_L$ on the order of $p^{d}$ where $p$ is the physical qubit error rate, compared to $p^{d/2}$ for unknown Pauli errors. \cite{gu_fault-tolerant_2025}
    \item Higher thresholds: surface code 50\% erasure noise vs. 19\% depolarizing noise.
    \item Dominant noise source can be engineered
\end{enumerate}

\bigskip

For Pauli errors, the decoder must infer where the error has occurred based on the specific stabilizers of the code.
For erasure errors however, they provide the advantage of knowing exactly where the error has occurred,
rather than guessing which qubit was affected.

\medskip

This small advantage allows the maximum number of errors that can be corrected to increase:
\[
    \frac{d - 1}{2} \quad \text{(for surface codes with Pauli errors)}
    \qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
    d - 1 \quad \text{(for surface codes with erasure errors)}.
\]

\medskip

As a result, we can expect the logical error rate for erasure errors to be improved, where the logical error rate
for Pauli errors is in the order of \(p^d\), and for erasure errors the logical error rate would be on the scale of \(p^{d/2}\).

\medskip

Another consequence of the higher correction rate is the noise thresholds. Surface codes can accept up to 19\% depolarizing noise.
With erasure errors, the threshold increases to 50\%.

\medskip

Most platforms (neutral atoms, trapped ions, NV centers, photonic qubits, etc.) can either naturally produce erasures
or can convert unknown errors into erasures through a platform-specific technique. Because many hardwares naturally exhibit erasures,
the higher erasure thresholds are practically relevant. \cite{gu_fault-tolerant_2025}


\paragraphsc{Challenges.}
Challenges from "The benefits and costs of quantum error correction with erasure qubits" (QIP 2025):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item More physical operations.
    \item Erasure detection imperfect, introducing another dimension to error models.
    \item Midcircuit erasure checks can create correlated noise for neighboring qubits
    \item Tools to simulate and decode erasure circuits are active area of research.
\end{enumerate}

\bigskip

While erasure errors have a myriad of benefits, they also prove to bring some challenges: Erasure errors often require extra physical operations. Erasure checks consist of checking if a qubit is in a known, non-computational subspace, and if it is determined that the atom has experienced an erasure error, then erasure resets will bring qubits who are in a known, non-computational subspace back into a computational space. Whether the atom has been lost, or whether it has entered an invalid energy level will determine how the erasure reset mechanism functions. Another difficulty that presents itself with erasure errors is that they can spread through entangling operations as Pauli errors. If a qubit has experienced an erasure error but it has not yet been detected by erasure checks, the error will propagate onto neighboring qubits. \cite{qip2025_shouzhen_2025}

\subsection{Surface Code}
Quantum error correction (QEC) assumes a piece of quantum information is distributed between many entangled physical qubits, realizing a logical qubit with higher fidelity than its constiuent physical qubits. Any errors affecting individual physical qubits can be detected and corrected with an ensemble of stabilizing codes, based on the diagnostic syndrome of the stabilizer code.

The surface code implements quantum errror correction on a scalable array of qubits using tessalatable bit and phase flip syndrome measurements. As shown in the figure below, each plaquette is associated with either an Pauli X or a Pauli Z syndrome measurement on the qubits in the corners of the plaquette. If a single qubit bit flip error occurs on a physical qubit, then the adjoining Z plaquettes will return a syndrome measurement of -1, creating a unique pattern that can be used to identify the erring qubit.

\paragraphsc{Circuit-level Noise Thresholds.}
If physical qubit error is below a critical noise threshold, the logical error rate should be supressed exponentially as the number of physical qubits per logical qubit is increased. In the surface code, the approximate relation between $d$ code distance corresponding to $2d^2 - 1$ physical qubits per logical qubit, the physical error rate $p$, logical error rate $E_d$, and error rate threshold $p_{thr}$  \cite{acharya_quantum_2025}.

\paragraph{Erasure Tolerance and Enhancements}

If left uncorrected, erasure errors pose a existential threat to encoded logical qubits that utilize the surface code encoding schema. While it is possible to compensate to some degree for erasure errors via the introduction of superstabilizers, the accumulation of erasures will eventually render your state unreconstructable if left unchecked. Kobayashi et al showed that a probability of erasure as low as 0.1\% can lead to logical failure rates increasing with code distance in regimes where the Pauli error rate is less than or on the order of 0.1\%. This suggests that to build a functional quantum computer, using the standard surface code encoding and the minimum weight perfect matching algorithm, one would need $p_{erase} << p_{pauli}$.
\cite{kobayashi_erasure-tolerance_2025}

\begin{align}
    E_d & \propto \dfrac{p}{p_{thr}}^{(d+1)/2} & \quad \text{[Logical error rate]}
\end{align}

Therefore when $p << p_{thr}$, the logical error rate is supressed exponentially with code distance. The exponential supression factor $\Lambda$ is approximately the linear relation between $p_{thr}$ and $p$: \cite{acharya_quantum_2025}.

\begin{align}
    \Lambda & = \dfrac{E_d}{E_{d+1}} \approx  \dfrac{p_{thr}}{p} & \quad \text{[Supression factor]}
\end{align}

\subsection{Formalism}
Our assumptions when simulating stabilizer circuits with erasure channels follow the formalism laid out in Fault-tolerant quantum architectures based on erasure qubits by Gu et al.  Specifically, we assume that all circuit noise is characterized by $p_{erasure}$ and $p_{depolarization}$, ignoring the $p_{classical bit flip}$ that the paper mentions, and that two qubit gates between an erased qubit $q_e$, and a qubit in the computational subspace $q_c$, results in $q_c$ becoming a maximally mixed state, which is possible because of the paper's assumption that coherence between the erasure subspace and the computational subspace is not possible. The simulation differs from standard surface code simulations primarily because of the inclusion of erasure locations within the simulated circuit. 
\cite{gu_fault-tolerant_2025}

\paragraphsc{Simulating Erasure Circuits}

Stim efficiently simulates stabilizer circuits containing exclusively clifford gates by computing the inverse stabilizer tableau of the circuit for each stabilizer measurement $Z_q$, where $Z_q$ corresponds to a measurement in the Z basis on qubit q. What this means, is that when given a clifford circuit C and a stabilizer code, rather than simulating the actions of C on qubits directly, what stim does is it computes the inverse change of basis with respect to C, C$Z_q$$C^{-1}$, to determine an equivalent measurement that can be conducted on the initial qubit state. Once Stim has generated a stabilizer tableau, when conduct subsequent noisy simulations of the same circuit, it conducts a Pauli frame simulation, which tracks bit flips and phase flips, and can be used in conjunction with the noiseless stabilizer tableau to simulate stabilizer circuits in O(g) time complexity and O(n) spacial complexity, where g corresponds to the number of gates and n corresponds to the number of qubits.
\cite{gidney_stim_2021}

\paragraphsc{Decoding}
When given an error syndrome for a logical qubit encoded using the surface code, the task of determining which error(s) occured is surprisingly nontrivial. The central issue with determining what error occured is that, while single qubit error syndrome patterns are unique, if you allow for multiple errors to occur in between or during syndrome measurements, there are multiple errors that can generate the same set of syndromes. Therefore, the problem of correcting an error measurement is not the problem of determining which error occurred, but rather is the problem of determining what is the most likely error to have occured. This problem can be solved efficiently using the minimum weight perfect matching algorithm...

It turns out that the problem of solving for the most likely error given syndrome is equivalent to a graph matching problem. Let us assume we are given a syndrome for a nine qubit surface code, such as the one given in figure 1, with the two middle Pauli Z plaquettes flagged. Because the $p_{n errors} < p_{n-1 errors}$ provided that $p_{error} < 50\%$, the most likely error to have occurred is precisely the error that acts on the least number of qubits. Therefore, while both an X error on the top left and bottom right qubits or an X error on the middle qubit produce the desired error syndrome, the most likely error is an X error on the middle qubit. 

To find the most likely correction for any two error syndromes first note that it is only possible to generate single stabilizer flags if an error occurs at a boundary not containing semicircle stabilizers of the same type. Define the X boundary as the boundary containing X semicircular stabilizers and the Z boundary as the boundary containing Z semicircular stabilizers. Assuming that a path is a chain of plaquette edges, let the minimum weight path between two plaquettes be defined as the path that connects a vertex touching each plaquette while touching the least number of vertices. Given two plaquette flags of the same type of a distance d surface code, the most likely error is given by the minimum weight path that connects either the two flags together, or both flags to the boundary corresponding to the Pauli Error that occured. 

Now let us say we are given n syndrome flags of the same type. Remember that if two syndrome flags are connected via a path, then applying a corrective action along that path addresses both flags. Thus, to find the most likely error, we need to find a configuration with minimum total path weight, where every flag is either connected to a unique other flag, or is connected to the requisite boundary.


\cite{Pesah_Introduction_to_Surface_Code_2023}
A hypergraph can be simplified to a normal graph if every hyperedge is connected to two vertices. In this particular case, the most likely error (MLE) problem can be reduced to a minimum-weight perfect matching (MWPM) problem. Through the blossom algorithm, the MWPM decode can solve the reformulation of MLE problems in polynomial time.

The MWPM problem is a simple graph which is built using only the defect vertices, which correspond to qubits that flipped according to the syndrome. Every pair of defects is connected by an edge. Each of these edges are given a weight, which corresponds to the cost of the smallest error path that could connect the two defects connected to that edge. In this way, MWPM finds out which error was more likely to occur. \cite{wu_minimum-weight_2025}

However, when we discard the assumption that uniform Pauli-errors occur, and instead the noise model used is biased towards a particular type of error, MWPM will experience a sharp drop-off in performance. MWPM does not account for bias by default. Since MWPJM pairs defects based on the shortest path / minimum weight, the most likely error in a biased noise model is not necessarily the shortest path.

As an example, if Z errors are 100x more likely to occur, and MWPM has the choice between two paths, one with five Z errors in a row, and one X error in the other, it will incorrectly pick the X error as the most likely error, since MWPM has no information about the error configuration.\textbf{Unsure whether to add citations to this}

\bigskip


Minimum Weight Parity Factor (MWPF) provides a hypergraph-based reformulation of the Most Likely Error (MLE) decoding problem. The purpose of MLE is to find the lowest-weight error configuration consistent with a given syndrome. However, the number of error configurations grows exponentially with the number of qubits, making MLE intractable; in fact, the problem is known to be NP-hard.

MWPF maps the MLE problem into a general hypergraph problem, retaining its intractability and NP-hardness. Although the conversion does not solve the NP-hardness	of MLE, the hypergraph conversion allows us to employ methods to approximate a near optimal solution in approximately polynomial time.

To employ this conversion efficiently, MWPF uses Hyperblossom, a generalization of the blossom algorithm for Minimum Weight Perfect Matching (MWPM) to a hypergraph equivalent. Hyperblossom is constructed with a primal-dual linear programming (PDLP) framework, which introduces relaxation and transformations to the hypergraph.

PDLP offers two major advantages. It allows for a certifiable proximity bound, allowing one to quantify how close the approximate solution is to the true minimum-weight parity factor. Second, given certain simple hypergraphs, it can find the exact minimum-weight parity factor solution in polynomial time.

To address the NP-hardness of the hypergraph, MWPF incorporates two techniques: clustering, and relaxation. Clustering exploits the locality of the errors by breaking the hypergraph into smaller subgraphs. Certain subgraphs, called relaxers, can be solved in polynomial time through Hyperblossom due to their simplicity. Subgraphs which cannot be solved exactly are handled using SingleHair, a process which assigns variables and hyperedges in a syndrome consistent manner. Local solutions from each cluster are combined into one global error estimate. Because the process breaks the decoding task into subproblems that scale with the locality of the errors rather than the number of qubits, the overall algorithm scales linearly with qubit number, achieving polynomial-time performance on average.\cite{wu_minimum-weight_2025}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\paragraphsc{Surface Code Threshold.}\label{sec:results-overview} We studied the performance of a rotated surface code under various noise and error syndrome decoding regimes (\autoref{tab:1}), with the goal of observing the impact of erasure conversion on our definition of the fault-tolerant error threshold $p_{thr}$. Using Stim \cite{gidney_stim_2021} to simulate the surface code stabilizer circuit, we perform Monte Carlo simulations of a $d \times d$ array of qubits representing an X-rotated surface stabilizer with code distance $d \in \set{3, 5, 7, 9, 11}$, physical error rate $p \in [0.001, 0.06]$, and erasure error conversion rate $R_e \in [0, 1]$.

Noise channels are applied to individual qubits \textit{before} each round (state preparation) and \textit{after} each Clifford gate operation with $p$ probability. We prepare the state $\ket{+}$ and estimate the logical error rate $P_L$ after $d$ rounds of measurements in the X-basis, assuming perfect syndrome measurements ($p_{measure} = 0.0$). As a baseline, we model standard Pauli noise by sampling errors uniformly at random from the set $\set{I, X, Y, Z}$, corresponding to the \verb|DEPOLARIZE1| (single-qubit) and \verb|DEPOLARIZE2| (two-qubit) simulated error channels.

We explore the impact of erasure error conversion by implementing the error model described in \cite{wu_erasure_2022} with some notable differences: each physical qubit experiences either a Pauli error occurring with probability $p_{pauli} = p(1-R_e)$ or an erasure with probability $p_e = pR_e$. Notably, we simulate $R_e=0$ and $R_e=1$, representing 0\% and 100\% erasure conversion respectively. We apply this noise model to \textit{all} Clifford gates (both one and two qubit gates). After an erasure error is detected, the affected qubit is reset to a maximally mixed state $I/2$. The resulting syndrome measurements are decoded to determine whether the shot can be corrected or results in a logical error.

We define $p_{thr}$ as the physical error rate $p$ where the logical error rate $P_L$ decreases as a function of $d$. To estimate $p_{thr}$, we identify crossing points in \autoref{fig:best-threshold-comparison}, where the logical error rate decreases as code distance $d$ increases, with the d=3,5 and d=9,11 crossings representing the lower and upper bound of $p_{thr}$ respectively. The highest $p_{thr}$ was 3.02\% when $R_e=1.0$ using the erasure-aware hypergraph MWPF decoder, compared to a 0.75\% threshold using the MWPF decoder in a pure Pauli noise regime. The procedure for circuit simulation, event sampling, and decoding is documented in \nameref{sec:experiment2} and \nameref{sec:experiment3}.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Min-weight Perfect Matching Baseline.} \label{sec:experiment1}
\paragraphsc{Additional Results.} See \autoref{fig:pymathing-pthresh}.
\paragraphsc{Data.} \footnotesize \href{https://github.com/leigh-johnson/C191A-qubit-minesweeper/tree/main/datasets/circuit%3Dsurface_code%3Arotated_memory_x/decoder%3Dpymatching}{datasets/circuit=surface\_code:rotated\_memory\_x/decoder=pymatching}
\normalsize
\paragraphsc{Procedure.} We consider $R_e=0.0$ with a purely Pauli noise regime. We apply a minimum-weight perfect matching (PyMatching) decoder, with the syndrome decoding process split between two phases:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Offline phase} (once per unique combination of swept parameters)

          For each choice of code distance $d$, physical error rate $p$, and erasure conversion rate $p_e$,
          we compile the Stim circuit into a \emph{detector error model} (DEM) \cite{gidney_stim_2021}.
          The DEM defines a collection of binary detectors $\{D_i\}_{i=1}^m$ and a collection of
          error mechanisms $\{E_j\}_{j=1}^n$. Each error mechanism $E_j$ is annotated with the subset of
          detectors it flips. This defines a binary parity-check matrix: \footnote{We understand the binary parity-check matrix as a Tanner graph, a type of bipartite graph used in classical low-density parity-check error-correcting codes. \cite{noauthor_tanner_2025}}
          \begin{align*}
              H \in \{0,1\}^{m\times n}, \qquad
              H_{ij} =
              \begin{cases}
                  1 & \text{if error mechanism } E_j \text{ flips detector } D_i, \\[4pt]
                  0 & \text{otherwise}.
              \end{cases}
          \end{align*}

          Each column of $H$ has Hamming weight 1 or~2, so we can represent the parity check matrix as a graph where column $j$ corresponds to either an edge between two detectors or a half-edge connecting a detector to a boundary node.
          Each column $j$ is assigned an edge weight $w_j$ derived from the probability $p_j$ of the error
          mechanism $E_j$: \cite{higgott_sparse_2025}
          \begin{align*}
              w_j \approx \log\!\left(\frac{1 - p_j}{p_j}\right).
          \end{align*}

          These data $(H, \{w_j\})$ are used to construct a \texttt{pymatching.Matching} object \cite{oscarhiggott_oscarhiggottpymatching_2025}.

    \item \textbf{Online phase:} syndrome measurement and minimum-weight decodings (per shot)

          During Monte Carlo simulation, each noisy circuit shot produces a binary syndrome vector:
          \begin{align*}
              \mathbf{s} \in \{0,1\}^m,
          \end{align*}
          where $s_i$ is the value of detector $D_i$ for that shot.
          If we let $\mathbf{x} \in \{0,1\}^n$ be some unknown binary error vector, with $x_j = 1$ indicating
          that error mechanism $E_j$ occurred. The detectors then must be constrainted to the solutions for:
          \begin{align*}
              H \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{s} \pmod{2}.
          \end{align*}

          For binary syndrome vector $\mathbf{s}$, \texttt{PyMatching} solves the minimum-weight decoding problem on the graph $H$ with the Sparse Blossom algorithm \cite{higgott_sparse_2025}, an implementation of MWPM designed for fast computations. The authors of Sparse Blossom explot the fact that the decoder only needs to predict which logical observables were flipped, rather than the physical errors.  \footnote{ For a textbook-quality explanation of decoding error syndromes in the surface code, see Craig Gidney's answer to StackExchange post: "Can you give an intuitive idea behind how the Minimum Weight Perfect Matching (MWPM) decoder works?"\cite{gidney_answer_2023-1} }. The result $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is mapped to a corresponding Pauli correction on the data qubits. We assume
          \begin{align*}
              \hat{\mathbf{x}}
              = \arg\min_{\mathbf{x} \in \{0,1\}^n}
              \left\{
              \sum_{j=1}^n w_j x_j
              \;:\;
              H \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{s} \pmod{2}
              \right\},
          \end{align*}
          The result $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is mapped to a corresponding Pauli correction on the data qubits.
          Comparing the corrected logical measurement to the ideal value determines whether the shot
          contributes to the logical error rate $P_L$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Experiment 2: Min-weight Parity Factor Baseline } \label{sec:experiment2}
\paragraphsc{Overview.} In this experiment we study the performance of the minimum-weight parity factor (MWPF) decoder
in the absence of erasure information, as an ablation against the MWPM baseline.
We assume a uniform Pauli noise model with physical error rate $p$ on each qubit and set the
erasure conversion rate to $R_e = 0$. The underlying Stim circuit and noise model are identical
to Experiment1; only the decoding algorithm is changed.  Notably, we do not use the version of the \verb|mwpf| decoder bundled with Stim in \verb|sinter\.decoders| and instead run our initial experiments with \verb|mwpf[stim]@0.2.12|. Later on, we incorporate alternative memory layouts in a fork of mwpf.
\paragraphsc{ Results.} See \autoref{fig:mwpf-pthresh}.
\paragraphsc{Data.} \footnotesize \href{https://github.com/leigh-johnson/C191A-qubit-minesweeper/tree/main/datasets/circuit%3Dsurface_code%3Arotated_memory_x/decoder%3Dmwpf/erasure%3D0.0/solver%3DSolverSerialJointSingleHair}{datasets/circuit=surface\_code:rotated\_memory\_x/decoder=mwpf/erasure=0.0/solver=SolverSerialJointSingleHair}
\normalsize

\paragraphsc{Procedure.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item {\textbf{Offline phase:} hypergraph detector error model and incidence matrix.}

          For each choice of code distance $d$, physical error rate $p$, and erasure conversion rate $p_e$,
          we compile the Stim circuit. We pass the circuit reference to the MWPF library via \verb|SinterMWPFDecoder|, which constructs the DEM \cite{wu_yuewuomwpf_2025}\cite{gidney_stim_2021}.
          The DEM again defines a set of binary detectors
          $\{D_i\}_{i=1}^m$ and a set of error mechanisms $\{E_j\}_{j=1}^n$. Unlike the biparite graph constraint used
          by \texttt{PyMatching}, the \texttt{MWPF} decoder allows each error mechanism $E_j$ to flip an \textit{arbitrary subset
              of detectors}, which is interpreted as a hyperedge: $F_j \subseteq \{D_1,\dots,D_m\}$. This defines a hypergraph parity check matrix, which is structured like an incident matrix having columns that can have \textit{more than} 2 non-zero entries. We compare with the incident matrix in Experiment1, where the columns had \textit{at most} 2 non-zero entries.
          \begin{align*}
              H^{(\mathrm{hyp})} \in \{0,1\}^{m\times n}, \qquad
              H^{(\mathrm{hyp})}_{ij} =
              \begin{cases}
                  1 & \text{if detector } D_i \text{ is incident on hyperedge } F_j, \\[4pt]
                  0 & \text{otherwise}.
              \end{cases}
          \end{align*}
          Each column of $H^{(\mathrm{hyp})}$ may have Hamming weight greater than 2, corresponding to
          hyperedges that touch more than two detectors, but in this baseline experiment all noise is still
          pure Pauli noise (no erasures), so many columns remain graphlike.

          Each error mechanism $E_j$ has an associated error probability $p_j$ determined by the circuit-level noise model. Like in Experiment1, assign a log-likelihood weight to each edge and pass \cite{wu_yuewuomwpf_2025}:
          \begin{align*}
              w_j \approx \log\!\left(\frac{1 - p_j}{p_j}\right),
          \end{align*}

    \item {\textbf{Online phase:} syndrome measurement and minimum-weight parity factor decoding.}

          During Monte Carlo simulation, each noisy circuit shot produces a binary syndrome vector
          \begin{align*}
              \mathbf{s} \in \{0,1\}^m,
          \end{align*}
          where $s_i$ is the outcome of detector $D_i$ for that shot. Again we define some unknown binary error vector $\mathbf{x} \in \{0,1\}^n$, with $x_j = 1$ indicating that error mechanism $E_j$ has occurred. The parity constraints imposed by the detectors can then be written as:
          \begin{align*}
              H^{(\mathrm{hyp})}\, \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{s} \pmod{2}.
          \end{align*}
          Given a measured syndrome $\mathbf{s}$, the MWPF decoder searches over subsets of hyperedges
          (parity factors) to find a minimum-weight explanation of the syndrome. This corresponds to solving
          \begin{align*}
              \hat{\mathbf{x}}
              =
              \arg\min_{\mathbf{x} \in \{0,1\}^n}
              \left\{
              \sum_{j=1}^n w_j x_j
              \;:\;
              H^{(\mathrm{hyp})}\, \mathbf{x}
              =
              \mathbf{s}
              \pmod{2}
              \right\},
          \end{align*}

          Although the solution structure may look similar to Experiment1, the solution set $\mathbf{x}$ are now all the parity factors whose incident hyperedges correspond to the observed detector parities. In the pure Pauli-noise baseline ($R_e = 0$), we understand this methodology to approximate the MWPM because in this special case each error mechanism flips at most two detectors.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraphsc{Reduced tolerance of errors in our surface code simulations}
\autoref{tab:1}.
In the case where depolarizing noise is modeled with purely Pauli errors, we reference the physical error threshold $p_{thr}$ published by \citeauthor{wu_erasure_2022} ($p_{thr} = 0.937(4)\%$) and \citeauthor{acharya_quantum_2025} ($p_{thr} \approx 1\%$) \cite{wu_erasure_2022}\cite{acharya_quantum_2025}.

In Experiment1, we visually estimated a much \textit{lower} error ($p_{thr} = 0.775$, \%err $=22.5\%$) for our X-rotated surface code with pure Pauli noise and the PyMatching decoder. We ruled out decoder implementation as a contributing factor (under this particular noise regime) after determining a similar result in our ablation study of MWPF in Experiment2 ($p_{thr} = 0.750$, \%err $=25.0\%$). We attribute this systemic error to multiple sources, in order of signifiance:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \autoref{fig:stim-circuit-gen}. We intended to assume perfect stabilizer measurements, but our circuit implementation applied the depolarizing error channel to each data qubit per measurement round.
    \item We applied the depolarizing noise channel to one and two-qubit gates at the same rate. Our references consider only two-qubit gates.
\end{itemize}

\paragraphsc{High Uncertainty in MWPF Experiments}
In our MWPF experiments, our Monte Carlo simulation was limited to 10,000 - 50,000 shots at a time (vs. 500,000 per single run in other trials). We aggregate the datasets from many (5+) in order to compare against other experiments. Fewer samples resulted in a wider confidence interval for MWPF samples, especially at low physical error rates (see \autoref{fig:mwpf-pthresh} and \autoref{fig:mwpf-pthresh-erase}). We attempted to gather enough data to make a fair comparison between our visual estimations for $p_{thr}$, especially for the sampling region around the threshold. However, the binomial fit method in our $P_L$ vs. $p$ plots is very sensitive to outlying datapoints. A visual estimate at the extrema of the very wide confidence interval for $d=7,9,11$ in \autoref{fig:mwpf-pthresh-erase} puts the $p_{thr}$ region anywhere from $\approx 3-6$\%. We report the most convervative value in our results table.

\paragraphsc{A Fun Sidequest: Profiling a Memory Leak}\label{sec:memleak-sidequest}
During our initial experiments with MWPF, we noted a memory leak during erasure-aware trials (see \autoref{fig:mwpf-memleak}). Using a combination of flamegraphs to analyze hot CPU/blocking GIL paths and a memory profiler to rank allocation requests by size/frequency, we realized \href{https://github.com/yuewuo/mwpf/blob/b8444428f999457208c4b0956f3f1c745a0ec2d5/src/python/mwpf/ref\_circuit.py}{ref\_circuit.py} and \href{https://github.com/yuewuo/mwpf/blob/b8444428f999457208c4b0956f3f1c745a0ec2d5/src/python/mwpf/heralded\_dem.py}{heralde\_dem.py} (quite reasonably) uses Python's functools.cached\_property decorator to cache computationally-expensive walks of the hypergraph model. The memory allocations are associated with a Python object instance and are freed automatically by a garbage collector when the Python object is destroyed. We noted RefCircuit instances holding memory allocations (via parent HeraldedDetectorErrorModel) even after the related sinter. Task had finished (but other tasks were still running). This is only a guess, as we were not able to patch and validate this particular hypotheses. Instead, we opted to side-step the issue by replacing some functools.cached\_property usage with functools.lru\_cache, which implements a last-recently used cache on per class definition (instead of per object-instance). As a result, we needed many more CPU cycles to complete our simulation but the memory footprint was contained to fit our availablae hardware (64 cores, 126GB DDR4 RAM, 112GB NVME-backed disk swap space).
