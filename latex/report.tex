\subsection{Noise Models}
\paragraphsc{Pauli Noise.}
The simplest model used to evaluate Quantum Error Correction (QEC) assumes all physical qubit errors can be represented by uniformly random Pauli operators applied during circuit simulation. In our simulations we implement a \gls{depolarizing error channel} to represent \gls{Pauli noise}, which has the following effect on some quantum state $\rho$ with $p$ probability of becoming a maximally-mixed state and $(1-p)$ probability of remaining unechanged:
\begin{align}E_{\text{pauli}}(\rho) & = (1-p)\rho + \dfrac{p}{3}(X\rho X + Y\rho Y + Z\rho Z) & \quad \text{[Depolarizing error channel]}
\end{align}

Unfortunately, error models based only on \gls{Pauli noise} are not robust enough to describe the real-world noise that must be corrected to realize fault-tolerant quantum computation. [CITATION NEEDED]

\paragraphsc{Leakage.}
A \gls{leakage} occurs when a qubit ``leaks'' out of the computational subspace (described by a two-dimensional Hilbert space) into a state that can only be described with a higher-order Hilbert space. If leakage goes undetected, the error compounds over time as it spreads through a multi-qubit interactions. Leakage can be stochastically modeled with a 3-level system (qutrit) with orthonormal basis: $\set{\ket{0}, \ket{1}, \ket{2}}$. Leakage events are assumed to be discrete and occur independently with probability $p_{\text{leak}}$ at each gate operation acting on the qutrit \cite{suchara_leakage_2014}. The leakage error $E_{E_{\uparrow}}$ on the $j$th qutrit in the system can then be understood as:
\begin{align}E_{\text{leakage}}(\rho_j) & = (1-p_{\text{leak}})\rho_j + p_{\text{leak}}\ket{2}\bra{2} & \quad \text{[Leakage error channel \cite{suchara_leakage_2014}]}
\end{align}

Leakage-reduction units (LRUs) were proposed as a way to convert leakage errors into Pauli-type errors, based on a one-bit teleportation protocol that effectively moves the logical state from the physical qubit in the leaked state to a freshly-prepared physical qubit \cite{suchara_leakage_2014}.

All species of qubit experience leakage errors, but it's important to note the underlying \emph{physical} processes look and behave very differently despite sharing a common mathematical description.


Transmon leakage. Transmon single and two qubit gates rely on coherent microwave driving to transition between computational basis states. Two qubit gates such as the controll Z and controll X gates are the primary source of leakage in transmon qubits because of their temporary usage of states outside of the computational basis to induce phase and bit flips in the target qubit. For example, when applying a control Z gate on transmon hardware to the state {$\ket{11}$}, we temporarily force both excitons onto one qubit, forcing it into the second excited state, {$\ket{2}$}, and letting the other qubit fall into the ground state, {$\ket{0}$}, before allowing the system to relax back into {$\ket{11}$} with the intended phase shift applied. However, there is a nonzero chance that the system does not relax properly, and leaves one of our qubits in the {$\ket{2}$} state, causing a leakage error in our system. \cite{Yang_2024, Miao_2023}

Neutral atom / Rydberg leakage. Neutral atom entangling gates exploit Rydberg blockade, which is a quantum phenomenon that occurs when an atom is excited to a particular Rydberg energy level, which prevents nearby atoms from being excited to the same state. The excitation of the single valence electron in alkali metals like Cs, Rb results in a large effective atomic radius [CITATION NEEDED, but I think it's on the order of ~25E5], creating a large electric dipole moment.

\paragraphsc{Erasure.} An \gls{erasure} occurs when a qubit is longer detectable by the measurement device, so the quantum state information encoded by the qubit is effectively ``lost'' or ``erased''. Mathematically, we extend the qubit Hilbert space with some $\ket{e}$ that is orthogonal to the qubit state $\{\ket{0}, \ket{1}\}$. The erasure channel can be defined as:

\begin{align}E_{\text{erase}}(\rho_j) & = (1-p_{\text{erase}})\rho_j + p_{\text{erase}}\ket{e}\bra{e} & \quad \text{[Erasure error channel \cite{suchara_leakage_2014}]}
\end{align}

Photon loss is the dominant form of error in photonic qubits [CITATION NEEDED].

Leakage-detection units (LDUs) were developed to address leakage errors in superconducting qubits, by periodically coupling a data qubit to an acilla that flags when the data qubit leaves the computational subspace. The flagged data qubit can be reset/replaced during subsequent error-correction cycles, effectively treating the flagged qubit as an erasure error.

Neutral atoms / Rydberg arrays, each atom is arranged into a known position using optical tweezers. Although the lattice/trap is situated inside a vacuum chamber, some nonzero amount of background gas collisions or thermal energy transfer from the environment can eject an atom from its lattice site.

\subsubsection{Advantages, Challenges of Erasure Errors}

\paragraphsc{Advantages.}
From: "The benefits and costs of quantum error correction with erasure qubits" (QIP 2025)
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can theoretically correct more erasure errors than unknown Pauli errors.
          We can correct $d-1$ erasure errors compared to $\dfrac{d-1}{2}$ Pauli errors. As a result, we expect logical error rate $P_L$ on the order of $p^{d}$ where $p$ is the physical qubit error rate, compared to $p^{d/2}$ for unknown Pauli errors. \cite{gu_fault-tolerant_2025}
    \item Higher thresholds: surface code 50\% erasure noise vs. 19\% depolarizing noise.
    \item Dominant noise source can be engineered
\end{enumerate}

\paragraphsc{Circuit-level Noise Thresholds.}

\section{Results}
\paragraphsc{Surface code thresholds}
We studied the performance of a rotated surface code under various noise and error syndrome decoding regimes (\autoref{tab:1}), with the goal of observing the impact of erasure conversion on our definition of the fault-tolerant error threshold $p_{thr}$.

Using Stim \cite{gidney_stim_2021} to simulate the surface code stabilizer circuit, we perform Monte Carlo simulations of a $d \times d$ array of qubits representing an X-rotated surface stabilizer with code distance $d \in \set{3, 5, 7, 9, 11}$, physical error rate $p \in [0.001, 0.06]$, and erasure error conversion rate $R_e \in [0, 1]$.

Noise channels are applied to individual qubits \textit{before} each round (state preparation) and \textit{after} each Clifford gate operation with $p$ probability. We prepare the state $\ket{+}$ and estimate the logical error rate $P_L$ after $d$ rounds of measurements in the X-basis, assuming perfect syndrome measurements ($p_{measure} = 0.0$).

As a baseline, we model standard Pauli noise by sampling errors uniformly at random from the set $\set{I, X, Y, Z}$, corresponding to the \verb|DEPOLARIZE1| (single-qubit) and \verb|DEPOLARIZE2| (two-qubit) simulated error channels.

To explore the impact of erasure error conversion, we implement the error model described in \cite{wu_erasure_2022} with some notable differences: each physical qubit experiences either a Pauli error occurring with probability $p_{pauli} = p(1-R_e)$ or an erasure with probability $p_e = pR_e$. Notably, we simulate $R_e=0$ and $R_e=1$, representing 0\% and 100\% erasure conversion respectively. We apply this noise model to \textit{all} Clifford gates (both one and two qubit gates). After an erasure error is detected, the affected qubit is reset to a maximally mixed state $I/2$. The resulting syndrome measurements are decoded to determine whether the shot can be corrected or results in a logical error.

We compare four methods for decoding syndrome measurements, described in more detail in each experiment procedure section:

\begin{itemize}
    \small
    \item \nameref{para:experiment1}
          We consider $R_e=0.0$ with a purely Pauli noise regime. We apply a minimum-weight perfect matching (PyMatching) decoder.
    \item \nameref{para:experiment4}

          We consider $R_e=1.0$. We sample and decode heralded erasure events first, using a strategy adapted from \cite{gu_fault-tolerant_2025} to re-weight graph edges (representing error mechanisms) on a per-shot basis. Then, we decode any measurement syndromes resulting from the maximally mixed state with a minimum-weight perfect matching (PyMatching) decoder.

    \item \nameref{para:experiment3}

          We consider $R_e=1.0$. We adopt the hypergraph minimum-weight parity factor (MWPF) decoder described in \cite{wu_minimum-weight_2025}, which frames the problem of decoding syndrome measurements and erasure error locations as a hypergraph matching problem.

    \item \nameref{para:experiment2}

          As an ablation study, we consider $R_e=0.0$ with a purely Pauli noise regime and apply the hypergraph minimum-weight parity factor (MWPF) decoder.

\end{itemize}
