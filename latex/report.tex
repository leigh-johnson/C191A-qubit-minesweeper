\subsection{Noise Models}
\paragraphsc{Pauli Noise.}
The simplest model used to evaluate Quantum Error Correction (QEC) assumes all physical qubit errors can be represented by uniformly random Pauli operators applied during circuit simulation. In our simulations we implement a \gls{depolarizing error channel} to represent \gls{Pauli noise}, which has the following effect on some quantum state $\rho$ with $p$ probability of becoming a maximally-mixed state and $(1-p)$ probability of remaining unechanged:
\begin{align}E_{\text{pauli}}(\rho) & = (1-p)\rho + \dfrac{p}{3}(X\rho X + Y\rho Y + Z\rho Z) & \quad \text{[Depolarizing error channel]}
\end{align}

Unfortunately, error models based only on \gls{Pauli noise} are not robust enough to describe the real-world noise that must be corrected to realize fault-tolerant quantum computation. [CITATION NEEDED]

\paragraphsc{Leakage.}
A \gls{leakage} occurs when a qubit ``leaks'' out of the computational subspace (described by a two-dimensional Hilbert space) into a state that can only be described with a higher-order Hilbert space. If leakage goes undetected, the error compounds over time as it spreads through a multi-qubit interactions. Leakage can be stochastically modeled with a 3-level system (qutrit) with orthonormal basis: $\set{\ket{0}, \ket{1}, \ket{2}}$. Leakage events are assumed to be discrete and occur independently with probability $p_{\text{leak}}$ at each gate operation acting on the qutrit \cite{suchara_leakage_2014}. The leakage error $E_{E_{\uparrow}}$ on the $j$th qutrit in the system can then be understood as:
\begin{align}E_{\text{leakage}}(\rho_j) & = (1-p_{\text{leak}})\rho_j + p_{\text{leak}}\ket{2}\bra{2} & \quad \text{[Leakage error channel \cite{suchara_leakage_2014}]}
\end{align}

Leakage-reduction units (LRUs) were proposed as a way to convert leakage errors into Pauli-type errors, based on a one-bit teleportation protocol that effectively moves the logical state from the physical qubit in the leaked state to a freshly-prepared physical qubit \cite{suchara_leakage_2014}.

All species of qubit experience leakage errors, but it's important to note the underlying \emph{physical} processes look and behave very differently despite sharing a common mathematical description.


Transmon leakage. Transmon single and two qubit gates rely on coherent microwave driving to transition between computational basis states. Two qubit gates such as the controll Z and controll X gates are the primary source of leakage in transmon qubits because of their temporary usage of states outside of the computational basis to induce phase and bit flips in the target qubit. For example, when applying a control Z gate on transmon hardware to the state {$\ket{11}$}, temporarily force both excitons onto one qubit, pushing it into the second excited state, {$\ket{2}$}, and letting the other qubit fall into the ground state, {$\ket{0}$}, before allowing the system to relax back into {$\ket{11}$} with the intended phase shift applied. However, there is a nonzero chance that the system does not relax properly, and leaves one of our qubits in the {$\ket{2}$} state, causing a leakage error in our system. \cite{Yang_2024, Miao_2023}

Neutral atom / Rydberg leakage. Neutral atom quantum computers exploit the large effective atomic radius of Rydberg states to perform two qubit gates. When an electron is excited to a Rydberg state, the separation between the electron and the now ionized nucleus creates an electric dipole field, which shifts excitation energy of the Rydberg state, an effect called Rydberg blockade. Following the protocol from Jaksch et. al, to perform a control Z gate, apply a {$\pi$} pulse to excite the control qubit into a Rydberg state, apply a 2{$\pi$} pulse to the target qubit, and then apply a {$\pi$} pulse to deexcite the control qubit \cite{Jaksch_Fast_Quantum_gates_2000}. If both qubits are in the {$\ket{0}$} then the state will remain unchanged, but if exactly one qubit is in the {$\ket{1}$} state then a rotation by 2{$\pi$} and thus a phase shift of -1, will be applied. If both qubits are in the {$\ket{11}$} state then after the first pulse, the Rydberg blockade will prevent the second excitation and a phase of -1 will be applied. Finally apply a Pauli Z gate to both qubits to shift the states -{$\ket{01}$ and -{$\ket{10}$ to {$\ket{01}$ and {$\ket{10}$. Occasionally when applying this protocol, the Rydberg state will not decay back to the {$\ket{1}$} state, and will instead decay to an orbital state outside of our computational basis, causing a leakage error. \cite{morgado_quantum_2021}

\paragraphsc{Erasure.} An \gls{erasure} occurs when a qubit is longer detectable by the measurement device, so the quantum state information encoded by the qubit is effectively ``lost'' or ``erased''. Mathematically, we extend the qubit Hilbert space with some $\ket{e}$ that is orthogonal to the qubit state $\{\ket{0}, \ket{1}\}$. The erasure channel can be defined as:

\begin{align}E_{\text{erase}}(\rho_j) & = (1-p_{\text{erase}})\rho_j + p_{\text{erase}}\ket{e}\bra{e} & \quad \text{[Erasure error channel \cite{suchara_leakage_2014}]}
\end{align}

Photon loss is the dominant form of error in photonic qubits [CITATION NEEDED].

Leakage-detection units (LDUs) were developed to address leakage errors in superconducting qubits, by periodically coupling a data qubit to an acilla that flags when the data qubit leaves the computational subspace. The flagged data qubit can be reset/replaced during subsequent error-correction cycles, effectively treating the flagged qubit as an erasure error.

Neutral atoms / Rydberg arrays, each atom is arranged into a known position using optical tweezers. Although the lattice/trap is situated inside a vacuum chamber, some nonzero amount of background gas collisions or thermal energy transfer from the environment can eject an atom from its lattice site.

\paragraphsc{Advantages.}
From: "The benefits and costs of quantum error correction with erasure qubits" (QIP 2025)

    \item Can theoretically correct more erasure errors than unknown Pauli errors.

    \item Higher thresholds: surface code 50\% erasure noise vs. 19\% depolarizing noise.
    \item Dominant noise source can be engineered
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Surface Code}
Quantum error correction (QEC) assumes a piece of quantum information is distributed between many entangled physical qubits, realizing a logical qubit with higher fidelity than its constiuent physical qubits. Any errors affecting individual physical qubits can be detected and corrected with an ensemble of stabilizing codes, based on the diagnostic syndrome of the stabilizer code.

The surface code implements quantum errror correction on a scalable array of qubits using tessalatable bit and phase flip syndrome measurements. As shown in the figure below, each plaquette is associated with either an Pauli X or a Pauli Z syndrome measurement on the qubits in the corners of the plaquette. If a single qubit bit flip error occurs on a physical qubit, then the adjoining Z plaquettes will return a syndrome measurement of -1, creating a unique pattern that can be used to identify the erring qubit.

If left uncorrected, erasure errors pose a existential threat to encoded logical qubits that utilize the surface code encoding schema. While it is possible to compensate to some degree for erasure errors via the introduction of superstabilizers, the accumulation of erasures will eventually render your state unreconstructable if left unchecked. Kobayashi et al showed that a probability of erasure as low as 0.1\% can lead to logical failure rates increasing with code distance in regimes where the Pauli error rate is less than or on the order of 0.1\%. This suggests that to build a functional quantum computer, using the standard surface code encoding and the minimum weight perfect matching algorithm, one would need $p_{erase} << p_{pauli}$.
\cite{kobayashi_erasure-tolerance_2025}

\begin{align}
    E_d & \propto \dfrac{p}{p_{thr}}^{(d+1)/2} & \quad \text{[Logical error rate]}
\end{align}

Therefore when $p << p_{thr}$, the logical error rate is supressed exponentially with code distance. The exponential supression factor $\Lambda$ is approximately the linear relation between $p_{thr}$ and $p$: \cite{acharya_quantum_2025}.

\end{align}

\paragraphsc{Decoding}
When given an error syndrome for a logical qubit encoded using the surface code, the task of determining which error(s) occured is surprisingly nontrivial. The central issue with determining what error occured is that, while single qubit error syndrome patterns are unique, if you allow for multiple errors to occur in between or during syndrome measurements, there are multiple errors that can generate the same set of syndromes. Therefore, the problem of correcting an error measurement is not the problem of determining which error occurred, but rather is the problem of determining what is the most likely error to have occured. This problem can be solved efficiently using the minimum weight perfect matching algorithm...

It turns out that the problem of solving for the most likely error given syndrome is equivalent to a graph matching problem. Let us assume we are given a syndrome for a nine qubit surface code, such as the one given in figure 1, with the two middle Pauli Z plaquettes flagged. Because the $p_{n errors} < p_{n-1 errors}$ provided that $p_{error} < 50\%$, the most likely error to have occurred is precisely the error that acts on the least number of qubits. Therefore, while both an X error on the top left and bottom right qubits or an X error on the middle qubit produce the desired error syndrome, the most likely error is an X error on the middle qubit. This generalizes quite nicely to a surface code of arbritary distance. Assuming that a path is a series of plaquette edges, let the minimum weight path between two plaquettes be defined as the path that connects a vertex of each plaquette while traversing least number of vertices. Given two plaquette flags of the same type of a distance d surface code, the most likely error is given by the minimum weight path that connects the two flags. This is because each vertex corresponds to a qubit, and so the least number of vertices traversed is equivalent to the least number of pauli errors occuring. 

Now let us say we are given n syndrome flags of the same type. Remembering that connecting two syndrome flags via a path corrects for both flags, if given an even number of flags, the problem of finding the most likely error is then the problem of pairing nodes such that the number of vertices traversed along all paths is minimized. If given an odd number of flags, one just has to find the minimum weight pairing between n - 1 flags, as a single flag can always be a corrected by applying a Pauli operator to a single qubit.

We studied the performance of a rotated surface code under various noise and error syndrome decoding regimes (\autoref{tab:1}), to understand the impact of erasure conversion on the error threshold. We simulate a rotated surface code in Stim \cite{gidney_stim_2021}. We perform Monte Carlo simulations over a sweep of parameters: code distance $d \in \set{3, 5, 7, 9, 11}$, depolarizing error rate $p \in [0.001, 0.06]$, and erasure error rate $p_e \in [0, 1]$.

We estimate the logical error rate $P_L$ after $d$ rounds of measurements. We assume perfect measurements. Before each round and after each Clifford gate, we model depolarizing noise with uniformly random Pauli errors from the set $\set{I, X, Y, Z}$
In the cases where $p_e$ is nonzero, we convert a percentage of depolarizing errors to erasure errors with a known location. After an erasure error is detected, the affected qubit is reset to a maximally mixed state $I/2$.

The resulting syndrome measurements are decoded to determine whether the sample can be corrected or results in a logical error.
